6.2 Reverse Coding Interpretation

1. Why is a reversal inappropriate for q9? How could q9 be coded to determine whether the frequency was about right?

Reverse coding is inappropriate for q9 because the most favorable response, “About Right,” is in the middle of the scale rather than at either extreme. A new binary variable could be created where 1 indicates “About Right” and 0 indicates all other responses.

2. Why should q13 to q26 not be reversed or averaged into one score?

These variables measure awareness and use of different financial services rather than evaluations on a continuous scale. Reversing or averaging them could produce misleading results. Frequencies and percentages for each service would provide a more meaningful summary.

9. Data Preparation Narrative

NoteData Preparation Narrative

The Employees Federal Credit Union (EFCU) survey dataset was imported from SPSS into R using the haven package. The original dataset contained 128 observations and 34 variables.

A data dictionary was created to examine variable names, descriptive labels, response categories, data types, and distinct values. The questionnaire was used to interpret nominal, ordinal, and Likert-type response scales.

Several metadata corrections were performed. The descriptive label for q8 was corrected to identify loan interest rates. Missing response labels were added to service awareness questions (q13–q26), and selected Yes/No variables were updated to include the “No answer” category.

An analysis dataset was created using the 32 original questionnaire variables. Two precomputed employee performance variables were excluded to allow the composite measure to be evaluated independently.

Special responses such as “No Opinion” were treated as missing values. The original SPSS import already contained missing observations for q7 (10), q8 (21), and q37 (7). These values were preserved during data cleaning.

Four variables (q7, q8, q10, and q37) were reverse-coded to make higher scores represent more favorable evaluations. The transformations were verified using frequency tables comparing original and reversed values. Question q9 was not reverse-coded because its ideal response was located in the middle of the scale.

Reliability and factor analyses were conducted to evaluate two candidate composite measures.

Employee Proficiency (q3–q6) demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.866), with factor loadings ranging from 0.722 to 0.923. The single factor explained 63.3% of the variance. An Employee Proficiency Index was therefore created by averaging the four items. The index had a mean of 4.205 and a standard deviation of 0.594.

Operational Effectiveness (q30–q34) demonstrated lower internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.539). The one-factor analysis explained 22.7% of the variance, suggesting that the five items do not adequately represent a single underlying construct. Consequently, no Operational Effectiveness Index was created.

The analysis is limited by missing responses in selected variables, the ordinal nature of individual questionnaire items, and the possibility that operational effectiveness encompasses multiple distinct dimensions. These limitations should be considered when interpreting subsequent analyses.
